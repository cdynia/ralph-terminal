Copy the filings, not the influencers

We asked a simple question: who should Ralph actually follow? The loudest answer — profitable traders on X — turns out to be the one the evidence says loses money. The quiet answer, buried in SEC paperwork, has a real edge behind it.

The verdict. Social-media traders are education at best and exit liquidity at worst — Ralph reads them and copies none of them. Corporate insiders and members of Congress, however, are forced by law to publish their trades. That public paper trail is the signal the bot is designed around.

What following X traders actually returns

The largest study of finance influencers to date — 72 million posts from 29,000 accounts — graded each one by what following their advice would have earned (Swiss Finance Institute, SSRN). The results split three ways:

56%
"antiskilled" — following them produced -2.3% per month in abnormal returns
16%
unskilled — no measurable value either way
28%
skilled — +2.6% per month, but you can't tell them apart in advance

The cruel twist: the antiskilled accounts have more followers than the skilled ones, because confidence grows an audience faster than accuracy does. Betting against the average influencer was itself a profitable strategy (+1.2% monthly). Even the best graded track record on all of FinTwit runs about 64% call accuracy — wrong one time in three.

And that's the honest end of the spectrum. Atlas Trading, one of the largest trading communities on Twitter, ended with its founders charged by the SEC over a $100 million pump-and-dump run on their own followers. Across pump-and-dump schemes broadly, retail buyers averaged -28% per scheme.

Rule one, permanently: no trade is ever taken because a social-media account posted it. Accounts worth reading for context — Peter Brandt, Mark Minervini, Quiver Quantitative — stay in the study pile, not the signal engine.

The legal insiders: two public paper trails

U.S. law forces the people with the best information to disclose their trades. Both disclosures are free, public, and legal to follow — this is not insider trading, it's reading insider filings.

SEC Form 4 — executives buying their own stock

Officers and directors must report trades in their own company within two business days. Sales mean little (people sell for taxes, houses, divorces); open-market purchases mean one thing. The academic record (Cohen, Malloy & Pomorski 2012; Lakonishok & Lee 2001) says the edge is real but narrow:

Congressional disclosures — the STOCK Act trail

Members of Congress must disclose trades within 45 days. Copying those disclosures is now a live, regulated product category: Quiver Quantitative's Congress-Buys strategy reports ~34% CAGR since 2020 versus ~21% for the S&P, and the NANC ETF — which simply mirrors one party's disclosed trades — has beaten the S&P 500 by about 14 points since early 2023.

The study pile (education, never signals)

For learning the craft itself, the channels with long tenure, honest loss discussion, and no course-pitch business model:
